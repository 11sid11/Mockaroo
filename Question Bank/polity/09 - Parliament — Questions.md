# Parliament — Questions

> Source: [[../../polity/09 - Parliament|Parliament]] | Subject: [[../../_MOCs/MOC - polity]]

A 15-question MCQ set on the Indian Parliament — composition of Lok Sabha and Rajya Sabha, Speaker, Money Bill, Ordinary Bill vs Money Bill, Joint Sitting, Ordinance-making power of the President, parliamentary committees (PAC, Estimates, COPU), and key recent amendments. Calibrated to SSC CGL Tier-I standard.

Scoring: SSC CGL rules — **+2 correct, -0.50 wrong, 0 unattempted**.

@qpol.parliament.q1 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=9
The maximum strength of the Rajya Sabha (Council of States) is:

- A) 245
- B) 250
- C) 543
- D) 552
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The Rajya Sabha has a maximum strength of 250 members: 238 elected by State Legislative Assemblies (through STV) and 12 nominated by the President from the fields of literature, science, art and social service. Present actual strength is 245 (233 elected + 12 nominated). 5 seats are vacant for PoK and Aksai Chin.

**Trap:** A (245) is the *current* actual strength — students confuse the maximum with the present strength. D (552) is the maximum for the Lok Sabha (530 + 20 UTs + 2 Anglo-Indian, but the 2 Anglo-Indian seats were removed by the 104th CAA, 2019).

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Article 80 - Composition of Council of States (Rajya Sabha)|Article 80]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q2 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=9
The maximum strength of the Lok Sabha is constitutionally:

- A) 530 members from states
- B) 543 directly elected members
- C) 550 (530 from states + 20 from Union Territories)
- D) 552 (530 + 20 UTs + 2 Anglo-Indians, before 104th CAA 2019)
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Article 81 fixes the maximum at 550: up to 530 from States and up to 20 from Union Territories. The 104th Constitutional Amendment (2019) removed the 2 Anglo-Indian nominated seats. The present elected strength is 543 (after delimitation).

**Trap:** D (552) is the maximum *before* the 104th CAA, 2019 — students who read older books or remember "2 Anglo-Indians" pick this. Since 2019, the Anglo-Indian nominated seats have been abolished.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Article 81 - Composition of House of the People (Lok Sabha)|Article 81]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q3 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=9
Which House of Parliament is described as a "Permanent House" (never dissolved)?

- A) Lok Sabha
- B) Rajya Sabha
- C) Both Houses
- D) Neither — both are dissolved simultaneously
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The Rajya Sabha is a permanent House — its members have a 6-year term with one-third retiring every 2 years, and the House is never dissolved. The Lok Sabha has a normal term of 5 years but can be dissolved earlier.

**Trap:** A (Lok Sabha) is the trap because the Lok Sabha is the more "powerful" House — but it is the one that *can* be dissolved.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Rajya Sabha (RS)|Rajya Sabha]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q4 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=9
The Speaker of the Lok Sabha is elected by the members of the Lok Sabha from amongst themselves. Who presides over the joint sitting of both Houses of Parliament?

- A) The President of India
- B) The Chairman of Rajya Sabha
- C) The Speaker of Lok Sabha
- D) The Prime Minister
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

The Speaker of Lok Sabha presides over the Joint Sitting of Parliament (Article 108). If the Speaker is absent, the Deputy Speaker presides; in the absence of both, the Deputy Chairman of Rajya Sabha presides. The Chairman of Rajya Sabha (the Vice President) *cannot* preside over a joint sitting because he is not a member of either House.

**Trap:** B (Chairman of RS) is the dominant wrong answer — students remember him as the head of the RS and assume he presides over joint sittings. He explicitly cannot.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Article 108 - Joint Sitting of Both Houses|Joint Sitting]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q5 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=9
A Money Bill can be introduced in:

- A) Either House of Parliament
- B) Only the Lok Sabha
- C) Only the Rajya Sabha
- D) Joint sitting of both Houses
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Article 109(1) — a Money Bill can be introduced *only* in the Lok Sabha, on the recommendation of the President. The Rajya Sabha can only suggest amendments within 14 days; if it does not act, the Bill is deemed to have been passed by both Houses.

**Trap:** A (either House) is the dominant wrong answer — students default to the bicameral principle. Money Bill is the single most important exception.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Article 109 - Special Procedure for Money Bills|Money Bill Procedure]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q6 difficulty=tricky subject=polity chapter=9
Consider the following statements about a Money Bill:
1. The Speaker of Lok Sabha certifies whether a Bill is a Money Bill.
2. The Speaker's decision on this is final and normally cannot be challenged in any court.
3. A Bill that imposes a fine or a tax by a local authority IS a Money Bill.

Which of the above statements is/are correct?

- A) 1 and 2 only
- B) 1 and 3 only
- C) 2 and 3 only
- D) 1, 2 and 3
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

- Statement 1 ✓ — The Speaker's certificate on whether a Bill is a Money Bill is the operative test (Article 110).
- Statement 2 ✓ — The Speaker's decision is normally final. The Supreme Court has held that this is *justiciable* (in *Kerala v. Keral Pradesh* and others), so judicial review is available in extreme cases — but normally it is final.
- Statement 3 ✗ — A Bill that imposes fines or taxes by a local authority is **not** a Money Bill (it is specifically excluded from the definition).

**Trap:** Statement 3 is the trap — it sounds like the kind of tax-related provision that *should* be a Money Bill, but Article 110(2) explicitly excludes it.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Article 110 - Definition of Money Bill|Money Bill Definition]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q7 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=9
Which of the following bills CANNOT be referred to a Joint Sitting of Parliament to resolve a deadlock?

- A) Ordinary Bill
- B) Constitution Amendment Bill
- C) Bill on a subject in the State List
- D) Bill on a subject in the Concurrent List
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

A Constitution Amendment Bill under Article 368 cannot go to a Joint Sitting. A Money Bill also cannot. The Joint Sitting procedure (Article 108) is reserved for *ordinary* bills and bills on the Concurrent List where the Houses disagree.

**Trap:** C (State List subject) is the trap — students think bills on State List subjects cannot even be introduced in Parliament (except in special circumstances). But if introduced, a deadlock could theoretically arise; the real absolute exceptions are Money Bill and Constitutional Amendment Bill.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Article 108 - Joint Sitting of Both Houses|Joint Sitting Exceptions]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q8 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=9
The Public Accounts Committee (PAC) of Parliament has how many members, and what is its primary function?

- A) 22 members (15 LS + 7 RS); examines CAG audit reports
- B) 30 members (all from LS); examines budget estimates
- C) 22 members (15 LS + 7 RS); examines reports of Public Undertakings
- D) 24 members (16 LS + 8 RS); scrutinises departmental working
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

PAC has 22 members (15 from Lok Sabha + 7 from Rajya Sabha). It examines the CAG's audit reports and is often called the "watchdog" of public expenditure. The CAG is the "Guide and Philosopher" of the PAC.

**Trap:** C swaps the function — that describes the Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU), also 22 members but a different function. B is the Estimates Committee (30 members, all LS).

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Public Accounts Committee|PAC]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q9 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=9
The three financial committees of Parliament — Estimates Committee, Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU), and Public Accounts Committee (PAC) — perform distinct scrutiny roles. Consider the following statements about what each committee examines:
1. The Public Accounts Committee examines the CAG's audit reports on government expenditure.
2. The Estimates Committee examines whether public sector undertakings are run efficiently.
3. The Committee on Public Undertakings examines the estimates of various ministries to suggest economies.

Which of the above statements is/are correct?

- A) 1 only
- B) 2 and 3 only
- C) 1, 2 and 3
- D) 3 only
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

Only **statement 1** is correct. The **Public Accounts Committee (PAC)** examines the CAG's audit reports on government expenditure — it ensures that money granted by Parliament has been spent within the authorised limits.

The correct roles of the other two are *swapped* in the statements:
- The **Estimates Committee** examines the estimates (budget proposals) of ministries and suggests economies — NOT the role of COPU.
- The **Committee on Public Undertakings (COPU)** examines whether public sector undertakings (PSUs) are run efficiently — NOT the role of the Estimates Committee.

**Trap:** B and C are the trap — students who remember the names but not the roles get them swapped. The Estimates Committee deals with ministries' budget estimates; COPU deals with PSUs.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Financial Committees|Financial Committees]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q10 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=9
The Estimates Committee of Parliament consists entirely of members from which House?

- A) Rajya Sabha
- B) Lok Sabha
- C) Both Houses in equal proportion
- D) Nominated members only
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The Estimates Committee has 30 members, *all* drawn from the Lok Sabha. It examines the estimates included in the budget and suggests economies. The PAC and COPU have members from both Houses.

**Trap:** A (Rajya Sabha) is the dominant wrong answer — students assume every committee has both Houses. Estimates Committee is the unique exception in being LS-only.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Estimates Committee|Estimates Committee]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q11 difficulty=tricky subject=polity chapter=9
Consider the following Assertion–Reason pair:

**Assertion (A):** The President can promulgate an Ordinance on a subject in the State List when Parliament is not in session.
**Reason (R):** Article 123 grants the President the same legislative power as Parliament, and residuary legislative competence lies with Parliament.

- A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
- B) Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A
- C) A is true but R is false
- D) A is false but R is true
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

- Assertion A ✗ — The President cannot ordinarily promulgate an Ordinance on a State List subject. Article 123 limits ordinance-making to subjects within Parliament's legislative competence. A is false.
- Reason R is partly true (residuary powers lie with Parliament), but R does *not* justify A — it actually supports *why* A is wrong.

So A is false and R, although not the reason for A's falsehood, is independently a correct statement of legislative competence. The answer is C (A false, R true).

**Trap:** A sounds plausible in an "extraordinary circumstances" framing — students want to give the President emergency powers. But the Constitution is explicit.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Article 123|Article 123]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q12 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=9
The maximum life of an Ordinance promulgated by the President is:

- A) 3 months from the date of promulgation
- B) 6 weeks from the date of reassembly of Parliament
- C) 6 months and 6 weeks from promulgation
- D) Indefinite — until Parliament approves
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

An Ordinance ceases to operate at the earliest of (a) 6 weeks from the reassembly of Parliament, or (b) 6 months from promulgation — whichever is earlier. Effectively the maximum life is "6 months + 6 weeks" of grace.

**Trap:** B (6 weeks from reassembly) is the *trigger*, but the *overall* max is 6 months + 6 weeks. Students confuse the trigger with the cap. D is wrong — indefinite re-promulgation was held invalid in *D. C. Wadhwa v. State of Bihar* (1986).

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Max Life of an Ordinance|Max Life of Ordinance]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q13 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=9
What is the quorum to constitute a meeting of either House of Parliament?

- A) 1/4th of total members
- B) 1/10th of total members
- C) Half of total members
- D) 100 members
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Article 100(3) — the quorum for either House of Parliament is **1/10th of the total strength** of that House. If the quorum is not present, the presiding officer must adjourn the House.

**Trap:** A (1/4) and D (100) are wrong — but A is the trap because it sounds "reasonable." The Constitution specifically chose 1/10th.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Article 100|Quorum]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q14 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=9
Which of the following actions is the President of India constitutionally barred from taking on his own discretion?

- A) Returning a Money Bill for reconsideration
- B) Returning an Ordinary Bill for reconsideration
- C) Returning a Constitutional Amendment Bill for reconsideration
- D) Both (A) and (C)
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: D**

The President cannot return a Money Bill for reconsideration (it was sent to him on the aid and advice of the Cabinet and with prior recommendation). For a Constitutional Amendment Bill, the President must give assent — no veto power. For ordinary bills, the suspensive veto is available (but Parliament can repass and the President must then assent).

**Trap:** B is the trap — students remember the suspensive veto applies to "all bills" but forget it does not apply to Money Bill and Constitutional Amendment Bill.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#Veto powers of the President|Veto Powers]]
</details>

@qpol.parliament.q15 difficulty=tricky subject=polity chapter=9
In which year was the voting age for Indian elections reduced from 21 to 18 years?

- A) 1961 (by 21st CAA)
- B) 1971 (by 24th CAA)
- C) 1989 (by 61st CAA)
- D) 2002 (by 86th CAA)
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

The **61st Constitutional Amendment Act, 1989** reduced the voting age from 21 to 18 years. This followed widespread demand, especially after the 1986 "Adult Suffrage" debate. Article 326 (adult suffrage) was amended.

**Trap:** A (21st CAA, 1967) added Sindhi to the 8th Schedule; D (86th CAA, 2002) added Article 21-A (Right to Education). Both are unrelated and confuse students.

**See also:** [[../../polity/09 - Parliament#61st Amendment of 1988|61st CAA]]
</details>
