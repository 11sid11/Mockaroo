@qpol.supreme_court.q1 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=11
The present sanctioned strength of the Supreme Court of India (Chief Justice + Judges) is:

- A) 1 + 7 = 8
- B) 1 + 25 = 26
- C) 1 + 30 = 31
- D) 1 + 33 = 34
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: D**

The Supreme Court currently has a sanctioned strength of 1 Chief Justice + 33 Judges = 34. Originally (1950) it was 1 + 7 = 8. The strength has been increased over time as workload grew.

**Trap:** A (8) is the original strength — students confuse the present with the original. The number of SC judges has been raised many times since 1950.

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#Strength of Judges|Strength of Judges]]
</details>

@qpol.supreme_court.q2 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=11
The retirement age for judges of the Supreme Court of India is:

- A) 60 years
- B) 62 years
- C) 65 years
- D) 70 years
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Article 124(2A) — a SC judge holds office until the age of **65 years**. (High Court judges retire at 62, per the 15th CAA, 1963 — earlier it was 60.)

**Trap:** D (70) is the retirement age for the Chairman of NHRC; B (62) is the HC judge retirement age. Both are common distractors.

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#Tenure|Tenure]]
</details>

@qpol.supreme_court.q3 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=11
Which of the following is NOT an original jurisdiction of the Supreme Court?

- A) Disputes between the Government of India and one or more States
- B) Disputes between two or more States
- C) Appeals from the High Courts
- D) Enforcement of Fundamental Rights
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Appeals from the High Courts fall under the **appellate jurisdiction** of the SC (Article 132–136), not original. The original jurisdiction (Article 131) covers Centre-State and inter-State disputes. Fundamental Rights enforcement (Article 32) is sometimes described as a "drastic" jurisdiction but is not classed as original in the formal sense.

**Trap:** D is the trap — Article 32 gives the SC writ jurisdiction for Fundamental Rights, which sounds "original" but is actually a separate writ jurisdiction. C is the right answer because appellate jurisdiction is the *most prominent* of the SC's work.

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#Original Jurisdiction|Original Jurisdiction]]
</details>

@qpol.supreme_court.q4 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=11
The collegium system for judicial appointments was developed through which set of cases?

- A) First, Second and Third Judges Cases (1982, 1993, 1998)
- B) Kesavananda Bharati, Minerva Mills, Maneka Gandhi
- C) Golaknath, Sankari Prasad, Sajjan Singh
- D) ADM Jabalpur, S. R. Bommai, Puttaswamy
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

The collegium evolved in three SC judgments:

- **First Judges Case (1982, S.P. Gupta)**: CJI's opinion not binding; executive primacy.
- **Second Judges Case (1993)**: "Consultation" means concurrence; CJI + 2 senior-most judges recommend.
- **Third Judges Case (1998)**: Collegium expanded to CJI + 4 senior-most judges; binding on executive.

**Trap:** B, C, D all involve landmark SC cases but are not about the collegium. The 99th CAA, 2014 replaced the collegium with NJAC; the SC struck down NJAC in *Fourth Judges Case (2015)*, restoring the collegium.

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#Collegium System|Collegium System]]
</details>

@qpol.supreme_court.q5 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=11
The first High Court to be established in India was:

- A) Bombay High Court
- B) Madras High Court
- C) Calcutta High Court
- D) Allahabad High Court
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

The Calcutta High Court was established in 1862 — the first High Court in India (and one of the oldest in the Commonwealth). Bombay and Madras High Courts came in the same year under the Indian High Courts Act, 1861.

**Trap:** A and B are tempting because Bombay and Madras are also "old" HCs — but Calcutta came first chronologically (and predates 1861 as the Supreme Court of Bengal, 1774).

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#1st HC (1862): Calcutta High Court|Calcutta HC]]
</details>

@qpol.supreme_court.q6 difficulty=tricky subject=polity chapter=11
Consider the following statements about the High Court writ jurisdiction (Article 226):
1. It is wider than the writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court.
2. The High Court cannot refuse to issue a writ.
3. It extends only to violations of Fundamental Rights.

Which of the above statements is/are correct?

- A) 1 only
- B) 1 and 2 only
- C) 1 and 3 only
- D) 1, 2 and 3
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

- Statement 1 ✓ — The HC's writ jurisdiction is *wider* than the SC's because the HC can issue writs for Fundamental Rights *and* other legal rights, while the SC (under Article 32) can only issue writs for Fundamental Rights.
- Statement 2 ✗ — The HC can refuse; the SC (Article 32) cannot refuse.
- Statement 3 ✗ — HC writs extend to *both* Fundamental Rights and legal rights.

**Trap:** Statements 2 and 3 are the dual traps. Students often confuse HC and SC: SC *cannot* refuse (FR is guaranteed); HC *can* refuse (it is discretionary).

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#Article 226 - Power of HC to Issue Writs|Article 226]]
</details>

@qpol.supreme_court.q7 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=11
The first female Chief Justice of a High Court in India was:

- A) Justice Fathima Beevi
- B) Justice Leila Seth
- C) Justice Anna Chandy
- D) Justice Sujatha Manohar
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

**Justice Leila Seth** was the first female Chief Justice of a High Court — of the Himachal Pradesh High Court (1991). Justice Anna Chandy was the first female *judge* of a HC (Kerala, 1959). Justice Fathima Beevi was the first female *judge* of the Supreme Court (1989). Justice Sujatha Manohar was the first female Chief Justice of a HC? Actually no — Leila Seth is correctly credited.

**Trap:** A (Fathima Beevi) and C (Anna Chandy) are the trap — both are famous female judicial firsts but at different positions. Beevi = SC judge; Chandy = HC judge; Seth = HC CJ.

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#1st Female HC Judge|HC Firsts]]
</details>

@qpol.supreme_court.q8 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=11
The jurisdiction of the Supreme Court to give advice to the President on matters of law or fact is called:

- A) Original jurisdiction
- B) Appellate jurisdiction
- C) Advisory jurisdiction
- D) Review jurisdiction
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Article 143 — the **advisory jurisdiction** of the Supreme Court. The President can refer questions of law or fact to the SC, which then advises. The advice is *not binding* on the President.

**Trap:** A (original) covers Centre-State disputes; B (appellate) covers appeals from HCs. D (review) covers the SC's power to review its own judgments (Article 137). All are separate jurisdictions.

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#Advisory Jurisdiction|Advisory Jurisdiction]]
</details>

@qpol.supreme_court.q9 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=11
Article 230 extends the jurisdiction of a state High Court to certain Union Territories. Two UTs — Lakshadweep and Andaman & Nicobar — share a curious feature: they are island UTs but are attached to two *different* High Courts. Which of the following correctly identifies which High Court covers each island UT?

- A) Lakshadweep — Madras HC; Andaman & Nicobar — Kerala HC
- B) Lakshadweep — Kerala HC; Andaman & Nicobar — Calcutta HC
- C) Lakshadweep — Bombay HC; Andaman & Nicobar — Calcutta HC
- D) Lakshadweep — Kerala HC; Andaman & Nicobar — Madras HC
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

**Lakshadweep** falls under the **Kerala High Court** (geographically proximate to Kochi). **Andaman & Nicobar Islands** fall under the **Calcutta High Court** (because of historical administrative links to the erstwhile Bengal Presidency). This is why the two island UTs are *not* under the same HC — a common SSC trap.

**Trap:** A reverses both — students assume "island = same HC." C places Lakshadweep under Bombay HC, which is geographically absurd but is a known distractor. D attaches Andaman to Madras HC, which is wrong because the A&N's administrative heritage is from Bengal, not Madras.

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#Article 230 - Extension of Jurisdiction to Union Territories|Article 230]]
</details>

@qpol.supreme_court.q10 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=11
A judge of the Supreme Court or High Court can be removed from office only by:

- A) The President on the advice of the PM
- B) The President after an address by both Houses of Parliament passed by a special majority
- C) The Chief Justice of India on grounds of misconduct
- D) The collegium in a majority vote
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Article 124(4) for SC and Article 217(1)(b) for HC — a judge can be removed only by the President after an address by both Houses of Parliament, supported by a majority of total membership and by 2/3 of members present and voting, on grounds of proved misbehaviour or incapacity. The procedure is detailed in the Judges Enquiry Act, 1968. No SC or HC judge has ever been removed through this process.

**Trap:** A (President on PM's advice) is the trap — that's how other appointments work. Judges are insulated from political executive by the special-majority requirement.

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#Removal|Removal of Judges]]
</details>

@qpol.supreme_court.q11 difficulty=tricky subject=polity chapter=11
Consider the following Assertion–Reason pair:

**Assertion (A):** The Supreme Court is also called the "Guardian of the Constitution."
**Reason (R):** The SC has original jurisdiction in disputes between the Centre and States, and can declare laws unconstitutional through judicial review.

- A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
- B) Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A
- C) A is true but R is false
- D) A is false but R is true
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

The SC is the "Guardian of the Constitution" because it can declare laws unconstitutional (judicial review) and resolve Centre-State disputes in its original jurisdiction. Both powers are constitutive of the guardian role.

**Trap:** B is the partial-credit trap. Both statements are true and R *does* explain A — judicial review and original jurisdiction together constitute the "guardian" function.

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#Basic Pointers|Guardian of Constitution]]
</details>

@qpol.supreme_court.q12 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=11
A retired judge of a High Court wishes to practise law in any court. Under the Constitution:

- A) The retired HC judge can practise in any court
- B) The retired HC judge cannot practise in any court
- C) The retired HC judge can practise only before the Supreme Court
- D) The retired HC judge can practise only before subordinate courts
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Article 220 — a retired judge of a High Court cannot practise law in any court (other than the Supreme Court? Actually, Article 220 applies only to *permanent* judges of the HC; SC Article 124(7) applies a similar restriction to SC judges). Retired HC judges generally cannot practise in any court or before any authority in India.

**Trap:** A is the dominant wrong answer — students assume "retirement = freedom to practise." The Constitution specifically prohibits it to preserve judicial independence.

**See also:** [[../../polity/11 - Supreme Court and High Court#Article 220|Article 220]]
</details>
