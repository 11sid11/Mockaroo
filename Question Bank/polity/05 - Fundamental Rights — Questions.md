# Fundamental Rights — Questions

> Source: [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights|Fundamental Rights]] | Subject: [[../../_MOCs/MOC - polity]]

A 15-question MCQ set on Part III of the Constitution — six Fundamental Rights, Articles 12–35, Right to Equality (14–18), Right to Freedom (19–22), Right against Exploitation (23–24), Right to Freedom of Religion (25–28), Cultural & Educational Rights (29–30), Right to Constitutional Remedies (32), the five writs, and key amendments (44th, 86th, 91st). Calibrated to SSC CGL Tier-I standard.

Scoring: SSC CGL rules — **+2 correct, -0.50 wrong, 0 unattempted**.

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q1 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=5
Fundamental Rights are contained in which Part and Articles of the Indian Constitution?

- A) Part II — Articles 5–11
- B) Part III — Articles 12–35
- C) Part IV — Articles 36–51
- D) Part V — Articles 79–122
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Fundamental Rights are in Part III, Articles 12 to 35. Part II is Citizenship, Part IV is Directive Principles, Part V is The Union / Parliament.

**Trap:** A (Part II) and C (Part IV) are the most tempting distractors — both contain rights-related provisions, but they are not the Fundamental Rights per se.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#PART III: FUNDAMENTAL RIGHTS [Article 12-35]|Part III]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q2 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=5
Right to Property was removed from Part III of the Constitution by which amendment?

- A) 42nd Amendment, 1976
- B) 44th Amendment, 1978
- C) 86th Amendment, 2002
- D) 91st Amendment, 2003
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The 44th Constitutional Amendment (1978) removed Right to Property from Article 19(1)(f) and made it a legal right under Article 300-A (Part XII). Originally there were 7 Fundamental Rights; now there are 6.

**Trap:** A (42nd) added "Socialist," "Secular" and "Integrity" to the Preamble and is the most famous amendment of that era — students default to it for any 1970s question. The 44th specifically restored liberties curtailed by the 42nd.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Basic Pointers|Basic Pointers]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q3 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=5
Which Article of the Constitution is called the "Heart and Soul" of the Constitution by Dr. B. R. Ambedkar?

- A) Article 14
- B) Article 19
- C) Article 21
- D) Article 32
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: D**

Article 32 — the Right to Constitutional Remedies — was called the "Heart and Soul" of the Constitution by Dr. Ambedkar, because it provides the writ jurisdiction through which Fundamental Rights are actually enforced. Without it, the other rights would be empty.

**Trap:** C (Article 21 — Right to Life) is the dominant wrong answer because Article 21 has generated enormous case law and is "the most used" right. But the famous quote is about Article 32.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Article 32|Article 32]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q4 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=5
Article 14 says "The State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws within the territory of India." Which of the following correctly distinguishes the two phrases?

- A) "Equality before law" — positive concept borrowed from the USA; "Equal protection of laws" — negative concept borrowed from the UK
- B) "Equality before law" — negative concept borrowed from the UK; "Equal protection of laws" — positive concept borrowed from the USA
- C) Both phrases are borrowed from the UK
- D) Both phrases are borrowed from the USA
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

"Equality before the law" is a *negative* concept (no one is above the law) — borrowed from the UK and championed by A. V. Dicey. "Equal protection of the laws" is a *positive* concept (the State must treat equals equally) — borrowed from the USA.

**Trap:** A reverses the source countries. Students who remember "positive" and "negative" still slip on which concept goes with which country.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Article 14|Article 14]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q5 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=5
Which Fundamental Right prohibits discrimination by the State against any citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth?

- A) Article 14
- B) Article 15
- C) Article 16
- D) Article 17
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Article 15(1) prohibits the State from discriminating against any citizen on grounds only of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth. Article 14 is the general right to equality; Article 16 deals with equality of opportunity in public employment; Article 17 abolishes untouchability.

**Trap:** A (Article 14) is the dominant wrong answer — students confuse the *general* equality guarantee with the *specific* anti-discrimination clause. Article 15 is the anti-discrimination clause.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Article 15|Article 15]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q6 difficulty=tricky subject=polity chapter=5
Match the writ with its meaning and typical use:

| Writ | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Habeas Corpus | (a) "We command" | (b) against unlawful detention |
| (ii) Mandamus | (c) "By what authority" | (d) compelling a public official to perform duty |
| (iii) Quo Warranto | (e) "To have the body" | (f) challenging the right to hold a public office |

- A) i-e-b, ii-a-d, iii-c-f
- B) i-a-b, ii-e-d, iii-c-f
- C) i-e-d, ii-a-f, iii-c-b
- D) i-c-f, ii-a-d, iii-e-b
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

- Habeas Corpus = "to have the body" (e) — against unlawful detention (b).
- Mandamus = "we command" (a) — compelling a public official to perform a duty (d).
- Quo Warranto = "by what authority" (c) — challenging the right to hold a public office (f).

**Trap:** C is the trap — it swaps the uses of Habeas Corpus and Mandamus. Students who remember the Latin phrases but not the use case confuse which "command" or "body" goes with which writ.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Five writs|Five Writs]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q7 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=5
The writ jurisdiction of the Supreme Court (Article 32) and the High Court (Article 226) differ in important ways. Which of the following correctly describes the difference?

- A) HC can refuse to issue a writ; SC cannot refuse
- B) SC can refuse to issue a writ; HC cannot refuse
- C) Both SC and HC can refuse at their discretion
- D) Neither SC nor HC can refuse; both are bound to issue writs
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

Article 32 makes writ issuance a Fundamental Right — the SC cannot refuse when a Fundamental Right is violated. Article 226 gives the HC a *discretionary* writ jurisdiction (it can refuse), and the HC's writ jurisdiction is *wider* because it can issue writs for violation of Fundamental Rights *and* any other legal right, while SC is restricted to Fundamental Rights.

**Trap:** B reverses the principle — students sometimes think SC has wider power because it is the apex court. The opposite is true: HC has *wider* jurisdiction but *discretionary*; SC has *narrower* but *mandatory*.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Writ Jurisdiction SC vs HC|Writ Jurisdiction]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q8 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=5
Article 21 of the Constitution — "No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law" — was taken from which country's constitution?

- A) USA
- B) UK
- C) Japan
- D) Ireland
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

The phrase "procedure established by law" in Article 21 was borrowed from the **Japanese Constitution**. (The US has "due process of law" — a *broader* standard, which the Indian founders deliberately did not adopt.)

**Trap:** A is the dominant wrong answer — students associate Article 21 with US-style due process because of landmark SC judgments that have effectively read "due process" into Article 21. But the *text* of Article 21 is Japanese.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Article 21|Article 21]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q9 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=5
Which Fundamental Rights are available only to citizens of India and NOT to foreigners?

- A) Articles 14 and 21
- B) Articles 15, 16, 19, 29 and 30
- C) Articles 23 and 24
- D) Articles 25 to 28
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Articles 15 (anti-discrimination), 16 (equality of opportunity in public employment), 19 (six freedoms), 29 (protection of minorities' interests) and 30 (minority educational rights) are reserved for **citizens only**. Foreigners can claim other Fundamental Rights (14, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25–28).

**Trap:** D (Articles 25–28 — Freedom of Religion) is the trap. Students assume religion-related rights are citizen-only because Article 25 uses the word "subject to public order" etc. — but the text starts with "subject to public order, morality and health and to the other provisions of this Part, all persons are equally entitled to freedom of conscience…" — all persons, not just citizens.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Rights that are available only to the citizen of India and not to Foreigners|Citizen-only rights]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q10 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=5
The 86th Constitutional Amendment Act (2002) made education a Fundamental Right for children of what age group?

- A) 6 to 14 years
- B) 5 to 12 years
- C) 7 to 16 years
- D) 6 to 18 years
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

The 86th CAA, 2002 inserted Article 21-A (Right to Education for children aged 6–14) and modified Article 45 (DPSP). The Right to Education Act, 2009 operationalises this. The 6–14 age band is the same as Fundamental Duty 51A(k) added by the same amendment.

**Trap:** D (6 to 18 years) is a tempting wrong answer — students extend the age band thinking it should match the broader "child" definition. RTE specifically covers ages 6–14.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Right to Education|Right to Education]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q11 difficulty=tricky subject=polity chapter=5
Consider the following Assertion–Reason pair:

**Assertion (A):** Mandamus cannot be issued against a private individual.
**Reason (R):** Mandamus is a writ directing performance of a public duty, and there is no public duty owed by a private person to compel performance of.

- A) Both A and R are true and R is the correct explanation of A
- B) Both A and R are true but R is not the correct explanation of A
- C) A is true but R is false
- D) A is false but R is true
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

Mandamus is indeed unavailable against a private individual (and also against the President, the Governor, and where the duty is discretionary rather than mandatory). The reason — that mandamus enforces a *public* duty — directly explains why it cannot run against a private person, who owes no public duty to be compelled by the State.

**Trap:** B is the partial-credit trap. Both statements are true, and R *does* explain A — they are not coincidental truths.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Mandamus|Mandamus]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q12 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=5
Article 17 of the Constitution abolishes untouchability. Which statute makes its practice a punishable offence?

- A) Article 14
- B) Article 15
- C) Article 17 itself (it is self-executing)
- D) The Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: D**

Article 17 abolishes untouchability, but to enforce it, Parliament enacted the **Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955** (originally called the Untouchability (Offences) Act, 1955). It is the statutory enforcement arm of Article 17.

**Trap:** C is the trap — many students assume Article 17 is self-executing like Article 14 or 21. Article 17 needs a statute to make the practice punishable; otherwise, it has no teeth.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Article 17|Article 17]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q13 difficulty=recall subject=polity chapter=5
Which Article empowers Parliament to restrict Fundamental Rights of members of the Armed Forces, Police and Intelligence Agencies for ensuring proper discipline?

- A) Article 31
- B) Article 32
- C) Article 33
- D) Article 34
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Article 33 empowers Parliament to restrict or abrogate the Fundamental Rights of members of the Armed Forces, Police forces, intelligence agencies and similar services in order to ensure proper discipline and discharge of duties. (Added/amended by the 50th Amendment, 1984.)

**Trap:** D (Article 34) deals with restrictions on Fundamental Rights *during Martial Law* — a different topic that is easily confused.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Article 33|Article 33]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q14 difficulty=apply subject=polity chapter=5
Article 22 of the Constitution provides protection against arrest and detention in certain cases. Under which of the following circumstances can a person be detained without being informed of the grounds of detention?

- A) Ordinary criminal proceedings
- B) Preventive detention under a law providing for such detention
- C) Arrest for a cognizable offence
- D) Never — grounds must always be communicated
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Article 22(4) to (7) deals with **preventive detention**. A person can be detained without being told the grounds for up to a limited period — but the Advisory Board must examine the case. In ordinary criminal cases (A, C), grounds must be communicated and the person produced before a magistrate within 24 hours.

**Trap:** D is the most "rights-respecting" answer and the trap. Preventive detention is the one constitutionally sanctioned exception to the general "grounds must be communicated" rule.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Article 22|Article 22]]
</details>

@qpol.fundamental_rights.q15 difficulty=tricky subject=polity chapter=5
Consider the following statements about Article 30 (Cultural and Educational Rights):
1. It applies to both religious and linguistic minorities.
2. The State cannot discriminate against a minority-run educational institution while granting aid on the basis of religion or language.

Which of the above statements is/are correct?

- A) 1 only
- B) 2 only
- C) Both 1 and 2
- D) Neither 1 nor 2
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Article 30(1) gives all minorities — religious *and* linguistic — the right to establish and administer educational institutions. Article 30(2) says the State, when granting aid to educational institutions, shall not discriminate against any institution run by a minority on the ground of religion or language.

(Note: Article 29 is *not* restricted to minorities — it protects the interests of *any* section of citizens including the majority — but Article 30 is minority-specific.)

**Trap:** Statement 1 is often misread because students remember "minorities" without realising the article explicitly extends to both *religious* and *linguistic* minorities.

**See also:** [[../../polity/05 - Fundamental Rights#Article 30|Article 30]]
</details>
