@qenv.trophic.q1 difficulty=recall subject=Environment chapter=3
In the standard trophic-level classification, organisms that prepare their own food from inorganic substances using sunlight (or chemical energy) occupy the:

- A) First trophic level — Producers
- B) Second trophic level — Primary consumers
- C) Third trophic level — Secondary consumers
- D) Fourth trophic level — Tertiary consumers
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A** — **Producers** (autotrophs, mostly green plants) occupy **Trophic Level I** and capture solar energy via photosynthesis. All other levels are heterotrophs.

**Trap:** B is tempting because the word "primary" sounds important — but "primary consumers" are *herbivores*, not producers.

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#TROPHIC LEVEL INTERACTION]]
</details>

@qenv.trophic.q2 difficulty=recall subject=Environment chapter=3
Which of the following food chains is an example of a **grazing food chain**?

- A) Dead leaves → Woodlouse → Blackbird
- B) Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk
- C) Fallen log → Fungi → Bacteria
- D) Detritus → Earthworm → Beetle
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B** — The grazing food chain begins with **green plants (producers)** consumed by herbivores and then by successive carnivores — exactly the *Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk* sequence.

**Trap:** A, C and D are all variants of the **detritus food chain**, which starts with dead organic matter, not living producers.

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#Types of Food Chains]]
</details>

@qenv.trophic.q3 difficulty=recall subject=Environment chapter=3
Which type of ecological pyramid is **always upright**, regardless of ecosystem type?

- A) Pyramid of numbers
- B) Pyramid of biomass
- C) Pyramid of energy
- D) All three are always upright
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C** — The **pyramid of energy** is *always upright* because of the second law of thermodynamics: energy is lost as heat at every transfer, so each successive level has *less* energy. The pyramid of numbers can be inverted (e.g., desert ecosystem), and the pyramid of biomass is inverted in aquatic systems.

**Trap:** B is the most common wrong answer — biomass pyramids are upright on land but inverted in aquatic ecosystems.

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#3. Pyramid of Energy]]
</details>

@qenv.trophic.q4 difficulty=recall subject=Environment chapter=3
According to the **10% Law** of energy transfer (Lindeman, 1942), approximately what fraction of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next?

- A) 1%
- B) 10%
- C) 50%
- D) 90%
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B** — Roughly **10%** of the energy at one trophic level is incorporated into the next; the rest is lost as heat, used in metabolism, or remains in undigested waste. This is why food chains rarely have more than 4–5 trophic levels.

**Trap:** A and D are exact complements of B — students occasionally confuse "transferred" with "lost" or "lost" with "transferred."

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#3. Pyramid of Energy]]
</details>

@qenv.trophic.q5 difficulty=apply subject=Environment chapter=3
In a pond ecosystem, the pyramid of biomass is **inverted** — phytoplankton (producers) have a *smaller* standing biomass than the zooplankton and fish that feed on them. The most accurate explanation is:

- A) Producers reproduce faster than they are consumed
- B) Fish biomass is incorrectly measured in aquatic systems
- C) Zooplankton and fish store more energy per gram than phytoplankton
- D) Phytoplankton have very short lifespans and rapid turnover
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: D** — Phytoplankton reproduce and are consumed so quickly that at any *instant* (a "snapshot"), their standing biomass is small — but their *productivity* is enormous. The inverted biomass pyramid therefore reflects rapid turnover, not productivity. So even though standing biomass is small, phytoplankton can support a larger biomass of consumers above them.

**Trap:** B is a procedural distractor ("it's a measurement error"), not a real explanation. C misrepresents metabolism.

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#2. Pyramid of Biomass]]
</details>

@qenv.trophic.q6 difficulty=apply subject=Environment chapter=3
A farmer sprays a pesticide on a field. Traces of the pesticide are found in grass, more in the grasshoppers that eat the grass, and the highest concentration in the hawks that eat the grasshoppers. This phenomenon — increasing toxin concentration up the food chain — is called:

- A) Bioaccumulation
- B) Biomagnification
- C) Bioremediation
- D) Eutrophication
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B** — **Biomagnification** is the *increasing concentration* of a non-degradable pollutant (e.g., DDT, mercury) as it moves *up* trophic levels. Bioaccumulation refers to a pollutant building up *within* a single organism over time; bioremediation is the clean-up of pollution; eutrophication is nutrient-driven oxygen depletion.

**Trap:** A is the most common wrong answer — bioaccumulation and biomagnification are closely related, but the chapter defines them separately and tests the distinction.

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#POLLUTANTS AND TROPHIC LEVEL]]
</details>

@qenv.trophic.q7 difficulty=apply subject=Environment chapter=3
A student is shown a diagram with multiple interlinked arrows among grass, mice, grasshoppers, frogs, snakes and hawks, instead of a single linear sequence. The most appropriate label for such a diagram is:

- A) Food chain
- B) Food web
- C) Energy ladder
- D) Trophic cascade
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B** — A **food web** is an accumulation of many interconnected food chains — exactly what multiple interlinked arrows represent. A food chain is linear (single pathway); a trophic cascade is a top-down effect, not a diagram type.

**Trap:** A is the most common wrong answer because students default to "chain" whenever feeding is involved — but multiple linkages between species is the *defining* feature of a web.

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#FOOD WEB]]
</details>

@qenv.trophic.q8 difficulty=apply subject=Environment chapter=3
A fallen log in a forest is broken into smaller fragments by beetles and worms, then water-soluble nutrients leach out, microbes break down complex compounds into simpler substances, and finally the residue is converted into dark amorphous humus and then inorganic nutrients. This sequence corresponds to which set of decomposition stages (in order)?

- A) Catabolism → Fragmentation → Leaching → Humification → Mineralisation
- B) Fragmentation → Leaching → Catabolism → Humification → Mineralisation
- C) Leaching → Fragmentation → Humification → Catabolism → Mineralisation
- D) Fragmentation → Catabolism → Leaching → Humification → Mineralisation
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B** — The chapter lists the stages as: **Fragmentation** (physical break-up) → **Leaching** (water-soluble nutrients wash away) → **Catabolism** (microbial breakdown) → **Humification** (formation of humus) → **Mineralisation** (humus → inorganic nutrients).

**Trap:** A and D scramble the first three steps; C starts with leaching, which is wrong because the material must first be broken into smaller pieces.

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#STAGES OF DECOMPOSITION]]
</details>

@qenv.trophic.q9 difficulty=apply subject=Environment chapter=3
A particular terrestrial ecosystem has the following biomass values (g/m², dry weight):

| Trophic level | Biomass |
|---|---|
| Producers (shrubs) | 20,000 |
| Primary consumers (deer, insects) | 2,000 |
| Secondary consumers (foxes, owls) | 100 |

Based on these values, the pyramid of biomass for this ecosystem is:

- A) Inverted
- B) Upright
- C) Spindle-shaped
- D) Cannot be determined from the data
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B** — The values decrease as we move from producers (20,000) → primary consumers (2,000) → secondary consumers (100), forming a classic **upright** pyramid of biomass. This is the typical terrestrial pattern. Aquatic ecosystems are the textbook *exception* (inverted biomass pyramid).

**Trap:** A is the classic wrong answer for students who have just learned that biomass pyramids can be inverted — but the inversion applies specifically to *aquatic* ecosystems, not this terrestrial one.

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#2. Pyramid of Biomass]]
</details>

@qenv.trophic.q10 difficulty=tricky subject=Environment chapter=3
Consider the following statements about ecological pyramids:
1. The concept of ecological pyramids was developed by **Charles Elton** in 1927.
2. The pyramid of numbers in a **grassland** ecosystem is typically upright.
3. The pyramid of numbers in a **desert** ecosystem is typically upright as well.
4. The pyramid of energy is always upright because energy is lost as heat at each step.

Which of the above statements are correct?

- A) 1, 2 and 4 only
- B) 1 and 4 only
- C) 2, 3 and 4 only
- D) 1, 2, 3 and 4
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A** — Statements 1, 2 and 4 are correct. Statement 3 is **false** — the pyramid of numbers is *inverted* in a **desert** ecosystem (a few large producers like trees support many small herbivorous insects, which support fewer parasites, etc.). Grasslands have an upright pyramid of numbers.

**Trap:** Statement 3 is the strong distractor because students lump grassland and desert together as "terrestrial" — but the chapter explicitly singles out deserts as an exception.

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#ECOLOGICAL PYRAMIDS]]
</details>

@qenv.trophic.q11 difficulty=tricky subject=Environment chapter=3
A textbook states: "A **food web** is more realistic than a **food chain** because it depicts the interconnectedness of food pathways." Which of the following statements best explains *why* a food web is a more accurate model of energy flow than a single food chain?

- A) Most organisms feed at only one trophic level, but a food chain overstates this.
- B) Most organisms feed at several trophic levels and on multiple species, which a single linear chain cannot represent.
- C) Food webs ignore decomposers, whereas food chains include them.
- D) Food chains apply only to aquatic ecosystems; food webs apply to all ecosystems.
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B** — Real organisms are generalists — a frog eats several insect species, a hawk eats snakes, rodents and other birds. A *single* food chain is a simplification; a *food web* captures this multi-prey, multi-trophic reality.

**Trap:** A is the opposite of the truth (most organisms are generalists). C is incorrect — food webs *do* include decomposers. D is false — both tools apply across ecosystems.

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#FOOD WEB]]
</details>

@qenv.trophic.q12 difficulty=tricky subject=Environment chapter=3
A farmer repeatedly sprays DDT on a crop. The pesticide remains in the soil for decades. Small amounts are absorbed by grass; grasshoppers eat the grass and accumulate the DDT; frogs eat many grasshoppers; snakes eat many frogs. The concentration of DDT is highest in:

- A) Grass
- B) Grasshoppers
- C) Frogs
- D) Snakes (top carnivores)
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: D** — DDT is a classic example of **biomagnification**: it is fat-soluble, non-degradable, and accumulates at *each* trophic transfer. Top carnivores (snakes) eat many contaminated prey over their lifetime and therefore carry the highest concentration.

**Trap:** C is tempting because frogs are "higher" than grasshoppers, but the *topmost* predator in the chain accumulates the most — that is the very definition of biomagnification.

**See also:** [[../../Environment/03 - Trophic Dynamics and Energy Transfer#POLLUTANTS AND TROPHIC LEVEL]]
</details>
