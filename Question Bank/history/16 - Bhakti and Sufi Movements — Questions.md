@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q1 difficulty=recall subject=history chapter=16
Which of the following saints is the founder of the Nathpanthi / Shaiva tradition that emphasised yoga?

- A) Ramanuja
- B) Matsyendranath
- C) Basavanna
- D) Kabir
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Matsyendranath (also spelled Matsyendranatha) is regarded as the founder of the Nathpanthi tradition, a strand of Shaiva mysticism that placed strong emphasis on yoga, breath-control (pranayama) and the guru-disciple relationship. Ramanuja (option A) was a Vedantic philosopher and founder of Vishishtadvaita. Basavanna (option C) founded the Lingayat movement in 12th-century Karnataka. Kabir (option D) was a 15th-century north Indian sant-poet, not a Nathpanthi founder.

**Trap:** Ramanuja and Kabir are easy to confuse with Nathpanthi yogis because both emphasised devotion and a personal inner path. But yoga-centred Shaiva asceticism is the Nathpanthi hallmark associated with Matsyendranath and his disciple Gorakhnath.

**See also:** [[../../history/16 - Bhakti and Sufi Movements.md#Content|Bhakti and Sufi Movements — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q2 difficulty=recall subject=history chapter=16
Ramanuja's philosophical system is known as:

- A) Advaita (non-dualism)
- B) Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism)
- C) Dvaita (dualism)
- D) Shuddhadvaita
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Ramanuja (trad. 1017-1137 CE), the foremost Srivaishnava theologian, propounded **Vishishtadvaita** (qualified non-dualism), which holds that Brahman, souls and the world are inseparable realities, like attributes of the one Being, while the world is real and grace (prapatti) is the path to liberation. Advaita (option A) is Shankara's non-dualism. Dvaita (option C) is Madhva's dualism. Shuddhadvaita (option D) is Vallabhacharya's "pure non-dualism".

**Trap:** All four options look similar because they end in "-dvaita", so reading the prefix matters: *Vishishta-* (qualified), *Shuddha-* (pure), *a-* (non-). A student who only remembers "Ramanuja = some kind of advaita" often picks A.

**See also:** [[../../history/16 - Bhakti and Sufi Movements.md#Content|Bhakti and Sufi Movements — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q3 difficulty=recall subject=history chapter=16
The Lingayat movement was founded by:

- A) Ramanuja
- B) Basavanna (at Kalyana)
- C) Madhva
- D) Kabir
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The Lingayat (also Lingayat/Virashaiva) movement was founded by Basavanna in the 12th century at Kalyana (in present-day Karnataka), the capital of the Chalukya (later Kalachuri) rulers. Basavanna rejected caste, Vedic ritualism and the authority of the Brahmins, and insisted that every devotee must wear the *ishtalinga* (a personal linga) on the body. Ramanuja (A) founded Vishishtadvaita; Madhva (C) founded Dvaita in Udupi; Kabir (D) was a north Indian nirguna sant, not a Lingayat founder.

**Trap:** Ramanuja and Madhva, like Basavanna, are 11th-13th century south Indian reformers, so students tend to mix up who did what. The Lingayat movement is exclusively associated with Basavanna and Kalyana, not with Srivaishnavism or Dvaita.

**See also:** [[../../history/16 - Bhakti and Sufi Movements.md#Content|Bhakti and Sufi Movements — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q4 difficulty=recall subject=history chapter=16
Which Sikh Guru established the Khalsa Panth in 1699 at Anandpur?

- A) Guru Nanak
- B) Guru Arjan
- C) Guru Hargobind
- D) Guru Gobind Singh
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: D**

Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Sikh Guru, established the Khalsa Panth at Anandpur Sahib in 1699 CE on the day of Baisakhi. He gave the Sikhs the five Ks (kesh, kangha, kara, kachera, kirpan) and the initiation rites of *amrit* and the surname "Singh" / "Kaur". Guru Nanak (A) was the founder of Sikhism. Guru Arjan (B) compiled the Adi Granth. Guru Hargobind (C) militarised the Sikh community after his father Arjan's martyrdom but did not found the Khalsa.

**Trap:** Several Gurus are associated with military or organisational reforms (Arjan built the Harmandir Sahib and Hargobind wore two swords, *miri-piri*), but only Gobind Singh actually instituted the Khalsa in 1699.

**See also:** [[../../history/16 - Bhakti and Sufi Movements.md#Content|Bhakti and Sufi Movements — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q5 difficulty=apply subject=history chapter=16
A student reads that a saint-poet rejected both Hindu ritualism and Islamic formalism, used local Hindi dialects, and his verses were collected in the *Bijaka*. The saint being described is:

- A) Kabir
- B) Guru Nanak
- C) Chaitanya
- D) Mirabai
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

The description fits Kabir (c. 1440-1518 CE). Kabir criticised empty ritualism from both Hindu and Muslim sides, preached a nirguna (formless) devotion expressed in popular Hindi dialects (Avadhi, Bhojpuri), and his verses were preserved by his disciple Bhago Das in the compilation known as the *Bijaka*. Guru Nanak (B), though he too preached a monotheistic path in Punjabi, is associated with the *Adi Granth* and the founding of Sikhism rather than the Bijaka. Chaitanya (C) was a Krishna-bhakti devotee in Bengal, not a nirguna critic of ritual. Mirabai (D) was a Krishna-bhakti poet-princess from Rajasthan.

**Trap:** Kabir and Guru Nanak both rejected ritual and used vernacular languages, so the giveaway here is the specific text mentioned — *Bijaka* belongs only to Kabir.

**See also:** [[../../history/16 - Bhakti and Sufi Movements.md#Content|Bhakti and Sufi Movements — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q6 difficulty=apply subject=history chapter=16
The Sufi shrine at Ajmer (Dargah Sharif) is associated with:

- A) Nizamuddin Auliya
- B) Moinuddin Chishti
- C) Salim Chishti
- D) Baba Farid
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The famous Sufi shrine at Ajmer, known as the Dargah Sharif, is the mausoleum of Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti, the founder of the Chishti order in India, who came from Persia/Chisht in Afghanistan and settled at Ajmer in the late 12th century. Nizamuddin Auliya (A) is the Chishti saint of Delhi, buried at the Nizamuddin dargah. Salim Chishti (C) is the Chishti saint of Fatehpur Sikri, not Ajmer. Baba Farid / Fariduddin Ganjshakar (D) is the Chishti saint of the Punjab-Haryana border region (Pakpattan).

**Trap:** Many students confuse the Ajmer dargah with Fatehpur Sikri because Akbar visited both. Only Ajmer, however, houses Moinuddin Chishti; Salim Chishti is buried at Fatehpur Sikri.

**See also:** [[../../history/16 - Bhakti and Sufi Movements.md#Content|Bhakti and Sufi Movements — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q7 difficulty=apply subject=history chapter=16
Which of the following is INCORRECT about the Bhakti Movement?

- A) It emphasised devotion over ritual
- B) It was led mostly by women and lower castes
- C) It was exclusively a North Indian movement
- D) It coexisted with, rather than immediately replaced, Sufism
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Statement C is incorrect: the Bhakti Movement was emphatically pan-Indian, not exclusively North Indian. Major centres existed in Tamil Nadu (Alvars and Nayanars), Karnataka (Basavanna, the Lingayats), Bengal (Chaitanya), Maharashtra (the Warkari sant tradition of Dnyaneshwar and Tukaram) and Rajasthan (Mirabai), as well as in Hindi-speaking north India (Kabir, Raidas, Tulsidas). Statement A is correct: devotion to a personal God was stressed over Vedic ritual and priestly mediation. Statement B is broadly correct: many Bhakti leaders were women (Mirabai, Andal, Akka Mahadevi) and from non-Brahmin or low-caste backgrounds (Kabir, Raidas, Chokhamela). Statement D is correct: Bhakti and Sufi traditions existed side by side for centuries, often influencing each other and producing shared practices and even shared shrine-sites.

**Trap:** Many textbooks emphasise the Hindi heartland (Kabir, Tulsidas, Mirabai), which leads students to imagine Bhakti as a North Indian phenomenon. But the earliest and arguably most influential Bhakti movements are the Alvars and Nayanars of Tamil Nadu, predating the North Indian sant tradition by centuries.

**See also:** [[../../history/16 - Bhakti and Sufi Movements.md#Content|Bhakti and Sufi Movements — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q8 difficulty=apply subject=history chapter=16
Match the following:

| Saint | Sect / Movement |
|---|---|
| 1. Mirabai | a. Vaishnava bhakti in Rajasthan |
| 2. Chaitanya Mahaprabhu | b. Krishna bhakti in Bengal |
| 3. Basavanna | c. Lingayat in Karnataka |

- A) 1-a, 2-b, 3-c
- B) 1-b, 2-a, 3-c
- C) 1-a, 2-c, 3-b
- D) 1-c, 2-b, 3-a
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

Mirabai (1) was a 16th-century Rajput princess-poet who devoted her life to Krishna, making her a classic Vaishnava bhakti figure of Rajasthan (a). Chaitanya Mahaprabhu (2), the 15th-16th-century Bengali saint, popularised ecstatic Krishna bhakti (the *kirtana* tradition) in Bengal (b). Basavanna (3) founded the Lingayat movement in 12th-century Karnataka (c). So the correct match is 1-a, 2-b, 3-c.

**Trap:** Chaitanya and Mirabai are both Krishna bhakti saints and are easily swapped because both are famous for devotional singing and *kirtana*. The decisive clue is geography: Mirabai — Rajasthan, Chaitanya — Bengal.

**See also:** [[../../history/16 - Bhakti and Sufi Movements.md#Content|Bhakti and Sufi Movements — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q9 difficulty=apply subject=history chapter=16
Who is the patron saint of the Chishti order in India?

- A) Salim Chishti
- B) Moinuddin Chishti
- C) Nizamuddin Auliya
- D) Fariduddin Ganjshakar
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The patron saint (and founder) of the Chishti order in India is **Khwaja Moinuddin Chishti** (d. c. 1236 CE), whose dargah at Ajmer is the order's central pilgrimage site. All the other Chishti luminaries mentioned (Salim Chishti at Fatehpur Sikri, Nizamuddin Auliya at Delhi, Baba Farid at Pakpattan) trace their spiritual lineage (*silsila*) back to Moinuddin Chishti. Farid was in fact a disciple of Nizamuddin Auliya, who was in turn a disciple of Baba Farid — illustrating the chain of authority originating with Moinuddin.

**Trap:** The most famous Chishti shrines in popular memory are those of Salim Chishti and Nizamuddin Auliya, so students may pick one of them. But "founder" and "patron saint" is unambiguously Moinuddin Chishti.

**See also:** [[../../history/16 - Bhakti and Sufi Movements.md#Content|Bhakti and Sufi Movements — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q10 difficulty=tricky subject=history chapter=16
Arrange in chronological order of birth:

1. Ramanuja (traditional dates 1017-1137 CE)
2. Kabir (c. 1440-1518 CE)
3. Chaitanya (1486-1534 CE)
4. Guru Nanak (1469-1539 CE)

- A) 1, 2, 4, 3
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3
- D) 4, 1, 2, 3
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

The correct chronological sequence is: Ramanuja (1017 CE) — Kabir (c. 1440 CE) — Guru Nanak (1469 CE) — Chaitanya (1486 CE). So the order is 1, 2, 4, 3. Option B (1, 2, 3, 4) is wrong because it places Chaitanya before Guru Nanak, but Chaitanya was born about 17 years after Nanak. Option C is wrong because it puts Kabir before Ramanuja. Option D is wrong because it begins with Guru Nanak, who is the latest of the four.

**Trap:** Kabir, Guru Nanak and Chaitanya are near-contemporaries of the 15th-16th centuries and all three are clustered together in popular memory. The decisive relative dates are: Kabir (c. 1440) is the earliest of the three; Guru Nanak (1469) is next; Chaitanya (1486) is the latest. Ramanuja, of course, belongs to the early 12th century and precedes all of them by 300+ years.

**See also:** [[../../history/16 - Bhakti and Sufi Movements.md#Content|Bhakti and Sufi Movements — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q11 difficulty=tricky subject=history chapter=16
Consider the statements:

1. The Bhakti movement often used vernacular languages to reach the masses.
2. Sufism in India was influenced by Hindu yogic practices but maintained its Islamic identity.
3. Bhakti and Sufi traditions were often hostile to each other in medieval India.

Which are correct?

- A) 1 and 2 only
- B) 2 and 3 only
- C) 1 and 3 only
- D) 1, 2 and 3
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

Statements 1 and 2 are correct; statement 3 is incorrect. (1) Bhakti saints deliberately composed in Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Marathi, Bengali, Hindi, Avadhi and other local languages rather than only in Sanskrit, in order to reach non-Sanskrit-literate audiences — this is one of the movement's defining features. (2) Indian Sufism, especially the Chishti and Mahdavi orders, absorbed yogic practices such as breath-control, sitting postures, meditation on the breath (*dhikr*), and even Hindu-derived musical and devotional idioms, yet it remained firmly within the Islamic tradition, drawing on the Qur'an and the Prophetic example. (3) is wrong: Bhakti and Sufi traditions in medieval India often coexisted peacefully, shared ideas and even shared shrines (e.g. syncretic figures like Kabir, or dargahs that drew both Muslim and Hindu devotees). Hostility is not the historical norm.

**Trap:** Statement 3 is a tempting answer because some Sufi texts did condemn Hindu *shirk* and some Bhakti texts condemned Islamic formalism, but this rhetoric of polemic is different from actual hostility on the ground. The lived medieval relationship was overwhelmingly one of coexistence and mutual influence, not hostility.

**See also:** [[../../history/16 - Bhakti and Sufi Movements.md#Content|Bhakti and Sufi Movements — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.bhakti_and_sufi_movements.q12 difficulty=tricky subject=history chapter=16
The famous work *Amuktamalyada* by Krishna Deva Raya deals with:

- A) The life of Vishnu
- B) Devotion (Bhakti) to Lord Venkateswara (Vishnu) — written in Telugu
- C) The court of the Cholas
- D) Persian grammar
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

*Amuktamalyada* (literally "a garland given to the devoted") is a Telugu *kavya* composed by Krishnadevaraya, the great Vijayanagara emperor (r. 1509-1529 CE), describing the devotion of the Alwar saint Andal towards Lord Ranganatha/Venkateswara (Vishnu). It is one of the masterpieces of Telugu literature and doubles as a treatise on bhakti (devotion), statecraft and morality. Option A is too vague: the work does not narrate the life of Vishnu himself, but the devotion directed to him. Option C is wrong: Krishnadevaraya fought the *Bahmani* kingdoms, not the Cholas (who had collapsed centuries earlier). Option D is wrong: Persian had nothing to do with it; the emperor himself composed in Telugu and Sanskrit.

**Trap:** The work's title *Amuktamalyada* is unfamiliar to many students, who may then guess either "life of Vishnu" generically (A) or pick a distractor tied to the emperor's military career (C). The correct answer hinges on knowing that Krishnadevaraya was himself a Telugu poet and a Vishnu-bhakta.
