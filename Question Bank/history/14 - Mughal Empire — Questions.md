# Mughal Empire — Questions

> Source: [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md|Mughal Empire]]

@qhis.mughal_empire.q1 difficulty=recall subject=history chapter=14
The founder of the Mughal Empire was:

- A) Babur
- B) Humayun
- C) Akbar
- D) Sher Shah Suri
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

The Mughal Empire was founded by Zahir-ud-din Muhammad Babur (r. 1526-1530 CE), a Timurid prince from Central Asia who defeated Ibrahim Lodi at the First Battle of Panipat (1526 CE) and then defeated Rana Sanga at the Battle of Khanwa (1527 CE). Humayun (option B) was Babur's son and the second Mughal emperor but lost the empire for a while to Sher Shah Suri. Akbar (option C) was the third and most famous Mughal emperor but he inherited the throne as a boy in 1556 CE, three decades after the founding. Sher Shah Suri (option D) is often confused with the founder because his brief interlude (1540-1555 CE) interrupted Mughal rule, but he was an Afghan, not a Mughal, and his dynasty was the Sur, not the Mughal.

**Trap:** Option B (Humayun) is the easiest wrong answer because the Mughal Empire is sometimes loosely said to have been "e-established" by him; but Humayun only restored the dynasty after losing it, he did not found it. Option D (Sher Shah Suri) is the trap for students who confuse the famous "interlude" ruler with the actual founder.

**See also:** [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md#Content|Mughal Empire — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.mughal_empire.q2 difficulty=recall subject=history chapter=14
The Battle of Khanwa (1527) was fought between Babur and:

- A) Ibrahim Lodi
- B) Rana Sanga
- C) Sher Shah Suri
- D) Hemu
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The Battle of Khanwa (17 March 1527 CE) was fought between Babur and Rana Sanga, the ruler of Mewar, near the village of Khanwa in present-day Rajasthan. Babur's victory decisively broke the Rajput confederacy's resistance and secured Mughal control over much of north India. Ibrahim Lodi (option A) was Babur's opponent at the First Battle of Panipat (1526 CE), not Khanwa. Sher Shah Suri (option C) defeated Babur's son Humayun at the Battle of Kannauj (1540 CE) and is associated with the Mughal interlude, not with Khanwa. Hemu (option D) was a general who briefly defeated the Mughals and crowned himself at Panipat in 1556 CE, almost three decades after Khanwa.

**Trap:** Option A (Ibrahim Lodi) is the most tempting wrong answer because Panipat 1526 and Khanwa 1527 happened in consecutive years under Babur and are often taught together; students must remember that Panipat was against the Delhi Sultanate while Khanwa was against the Rajputs.

**See also:** [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md#Content|Mughal Empire — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.mughal_empire.q3 difficulty=recall subject=history chapter=14
Akbar's policy of "Sulh-i-Kul" (universal tolerance) is associated with:

- A) His military conquests
- B) The Ibadat Khana and Din-i-Ilahi
- C) His land revenue reforms (Todar Mal)
- D) His mansabdari system
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Sulh-i-Kul (literally "peace with all" or universal tolerance) was Akbar's policy of religious pluralism, cultivated through interfaith dialogue at the Ibadat Khana (House of Worship) at Fatehpur Sikri from 1575 CE onwards, and culminating in the syncretic Din-i-Ilahi (the "Divine Faith") that Akbar promulgated around 1582 CE. Military conquests (option A) were a parallel and earlier activity, not the basis of Sulh-i-Kul. Todar Mal's land revenue reforms (option C) were a fiscal-administrative innovation, unrelated to religious policy. The mansabdari system (option D) was Akbar's military-administrative ranking system for nobles and officers, again a different domain.

**Trap:** Options C and D are both real Akbar-era reforms, so students who know Akbar but blur his many policies together can confuse which reform belongs to which category. The unique marker of Sulh-i-Kul is its religious, not fiscal or military, content.

**See also:** [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md#Content|Mughal Empire — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.mughal_empire.q4 difficulty=recall subject=history chapter=14
Aurangzeb reimposed Jizya in:

- A) 1564
- B) 1659
- C) 1679
- D) 1707
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Aurangzeb reimposed the Jizya (a poll tax on non-Muslims, abolished earlier by Akbar in 1564 CE) in 1679 CE. This was one of the most contested acts of his reign and is widely regarded as a turning point in Mughal-Rajput and Mughal-Maratha relations. Option A (1564 CE) is the year Akbar ABOLISHED the Jizya and is the trap for students who confuse who abolished/re-imposed it. Option B (1659 CE) is the year Aurangzeb executed his brother Dara Shukoh and consolidated power at the start of his reign, not the year of the Jizya. Option D (1707 CE) is the year of Aurangzeb's death.

**Trap:** Option A is the strongest distractor because Akbar's abolition (1564) and Aurangzeb's reimposition (1679) are mirror-image events; the question deliberately asks about REIMPOSITION, so the answer must be the later date.

**See also:** [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md#Content|Mughal Empire — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.mughal_empire.q5 difficulty=apply subject=history chapter=14
A student reads that an interlude of Mughal rule in India was led by an Afghan who built the Grand Trunk Road, introduced the silver "Rupia", and was eventually defeated by Humayun. The ruler being described is:

- A) Sher Shah Suri
- B) Babur
- C) Bairam Khan
- D) Ibrahim Lodi
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

The ruler described is Sher Shah Suri (Farid Khan, r. 1540-1545 CE), an Afghan of the Sur dynasty who briefly displaced Humayun and ruled from Delhi and Agra. Sher Shah is famous for: (i) rebuilding and extending the Grand Trunk Road from the Indus to Bengal, (ii) issuing the silver "Rupiya" coin that became the ancestor of the modern rupee, (iii) introducing a survey-based land revenue system that later influenced Todar Mal's reforms, and (iv) defeating Humayun at Kannauj in 1540 CE before Humayun returned to reconquer Delhi in 1555 CE. Babur (option B) founded the Mughal dynasty and was not Afghan. Bairam Khan (option C) was a Persian-origin regent who helped the young Akbar, not an interlude ruler. Ibrahim Lodi (option D) was the last Lodi Sultan defeated by Babur at Panipat in 1526 CE.

**Trap:** Option B (Babur) is the trap for students who confuse "Mughal founder" with "interlude ruler"; the question explicitly mentions the interlude period and Humayun's defeat, both of which point to the Sur interlude.

**See also:** [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md#Content|Mughal Empire — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.mughal_empire.q6 difficulty=apply subject=history chapter=14
Which of the following is INCORRECT about Akbar's administration?

- A) The Ibadat Khana was at Fatehpur Sikri
- B) The Mansabdari system had 33 grades
- C) Todar Mal's bandobast was a land-revenue settlement
- D) Akbar abolished the Jizya in 1679
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: D**

Statement D is INCORRECT because Akbar abolished the Jizya in 1564 CE, not in 1679 CE. The year 1679 CE is when Aurangzeb REIMPOSED the Jizya; mixing up the two rulers and the two events makes this the wrong option. Statements A, B and C are all correct: the Ibadat Khana was indeed constructed at Fatehpur Sikri in 1575 CE; the Mansabdari system organized officers into 33 grades (called zat and sawar ranks); and Todar Mal's "andobast" (settled assessment) of 1580-1582 CE was Akbar's famous land revenue reform based on careful crop and area measurement.

**Trap:** Option D is engineered to confuse the year Akbar abolished the Jizya with the year Aurangzeb reimposed it. Students who know that "	he Jizya was abolished by Akbar" but confuse the date will pick D as the answer; the date 1679 is the giveaway that this is the INCORRECT statement.

**See also:** [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md#Content|Mughal Empire — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.mughal_empire.q7 difficulty=apply subject=history chapter=14
Who was the last Mughal emperor?

- A) Bahadur Shah Zafar II
- B) Akbar II
- C) Aurangzeb
- D) Bahadur Shah I
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

Bahadur Shah Zafar II (r. 1837-1857 CE) was the last Mughal emperor. He was a titular ruler confined by the British to the Red Fort at Delhi and was deposed and exiled to Rangoon (Burma) after the 1857 uprising, in which he was proclaimed a symbolic leader of the revolt. He died in 1862 CE. Akbar II (option B) was the second-to-last emperor (r. 1806-1837 CE) and Bahadur Shah Zafar II's father. Aurangzeb (option C) died in 1707 CE, more than a century before the end of Mughal power. Bahadur Shah I (option D, also called Muazzam, r. 1707-1712 CE) was Aurangzeb's immediate successor and the first emperor after the long Maratha-Deccan wars.

**Trap:** Option C (Aurangzeb) is the most attractive wrong answer because Aurangzeb's death (1707 CE) is widely cited as the start of Mughal decline; but he was not the LAST emperor, only the last of the strong "Great Mughals." Option B (Akbar II) is the trap for students who know the next-to-last emperor but not the very last.

**See also:** [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md#Content|Mughal Empire — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.mughal_empire.q8 difficulty=apply subject=history chapter=14
Match the following Mughal rulers with their associated works:

| Ruler | Work / Building |
|---|---|
| 1. Akbar | a. Taj Mahal (commission) |
| 2. Shah Jahan | b. Agra Fort, Fatehpur Sikri |
| 3. Babur | c. Baburnama |

- A) 1-a, 2-b, 3-c
- B) 1-b, 2-a, 3-c
- C) 1-c, 2-b, 3-a
- D) 1-b, 2-c, 3-a
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The correct matching is 1-b, 2-a, 3-c. Akbar (1) is associated with Agra Fort (the red sandstone fort at Agra) and Fatehpur Sikri (the imperial city he built and largely abandoned), which is option b. Shah Jahan (2) commissioned the Taj Mahal at Agra as a tomb for his wife Mumtaz Mahal, which is option a. Babur (3) authored the Baburnama, his memoirs in Chagatai Turkish, which is option c. Options A and D misalign one or more rulers with their monuments or works.

**Trap:** Option A swaps Akbar with Shah Jahan in students' memories because Shah Jahan is more famous for architecture; students who remember "Akbar -> Agra Fort" and "Shah Jahan -> Taj Mahal" but forget the Baburnama column can pick A. Option C pairs Babur with Taj Mahal (clearly wrong) but otherwise mixes the pairings.

**See also:** [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md#Content|Mughal Empire — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.mughal_empire.q9 difficulty=apply subject=history chapter=14
The Deccan policies of Aurangzeb ultimately:

- A) Consolidated Mughal control and yielded a stable empire
- B) Drained the treasury and exposed the north to Maratha attacks
- C) Brought peace with the Marathas through treaties
- D) Led to a complete annexation of the Maratha kingdom
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Aurangzeb's long Deccan campaigns (1681-1707 CE), especially against the Marathas under Shivaji and his successors Sambhaji and Rajaram, drained the imperial treasury, tied down the best Mughal armies far from the north, and exposed northern India to Jat, Sikh and later Rajput-Maratha incursions. The Marathas were never fully defeated; they adopted guerrilla tactics, and by the time of Aurangzeb's death (1707 CE) Mughal control of the Deccan was nominal at best. Option A is the opposite of the historical outcome. Option C is incorrect because Aurangzeb refused to treat the Marathas as legitimate political equals and never made a lasting peace with them. Option D is incorrect because Aurangzeb captured and executed Sambhaji but never fully annexed Maratha territory; Shivaji's successors continued the fight.

**Trap:** Option A is tempting if students remember that Aurangzeb briefly captured Maratha strongholds; but the long-term effect of his Deccan campaigns was exhaustion, not consolidation. Option D is the trap for students who over-simplify Aurangzeb's campaigns as a "ictory over the Marathas."

**See also:** [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md#Content|Mughal Empire — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.mughal_empire.q10 difficulty=tricky subject=history chapter=14
Arrange the following in chronological order:

1. Babur's invasion
2. Sher Shah Suri's interlude
3. Akbar's accession
4. Aurangzeb's death

- A) 1, 3, 2, 4
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3
- D) 1, 2, 4, 3
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The correct chronological order is 1 -> 2 -> 3 -> 4. (1) Babur's invasion and the founding of the Mughal Empire occurred in 1526 CE with the First Battle of Panipat. (2) Sher Shah Suri's interlude ran from 1540 to 1555 CE, after he defeated Humayun at Kannauj. (3) Akbar acceded to the Mughal throne in 1556 CE (with Bairam Khan as regent) and ruled until 1605 CE. (4) Aurangzeb died in 1707 CE after his long Deccan campaigns. Option A reverses (2) and (3), placing Akbar's accession before the Sur interlude. Option C scrambles all four events. Option D places Aurangzeb's death before Akbar's accession, which is impossible.

**Trap:** Option A is the most attractive wrong answer because students often forget that Sher Shah's interlude (1540-1555 CE) actually came BETWEEN Babur (1526) and Akbar (1556); they assume the dynasty passed smoothly from Babur to Humayun to Akbar.

**See also:** [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md#Content|Mughal Empire — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.mughal_empire.q11 difficulty=tricky subject=history chapter=14
Consider the statements:

1. Akbar's Rajput policy was based on matrimonial alliances and strategic incorporation.
2. Shah Jahan's reign is called the "Golden Age of the Mughals" for architecture.
3. Aurangzeb's religious policies made him the most popular Mughal emperor with the people.

Which are correct?

- A) 1 and 2 only
- B) 2 and 3 only
- C) 1 and 3 only
- D) 1, 2 and 3
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

Statements 1 and 2 are correct; statement 3 is INCORRECT. Akbar's Rajput policy was indeed a policy of matrimonial alliances (he married Jodha Bai / Harkha Bai of Amer, plus other Rajput princesses) and strategic incorporation of Rajput chiefs as trusted Mughal nobles and governors; this pacified the Rajputana region and produced long-lasting alliances. Shah Jahan's reign (1628-1658 CE) is widely known as the "Golden Age of the Mughals" for architecture because of the Taj Mahal, the Red Fort at Delhi, the Jama Masjid, the Moti Masjid, and the Peacock Throne; the empire was at its largest territorial extent and richest in this period. Statement 3 is INCORRECT because Aurangzeb's religious policies (reimposition of Jizya in 1679 CE, destruction of temples, banning of music at court, etc.) made him UNPOPULAR with the Rajputs, Marathas, Sikhs and a large section of the Hindu population, contributing to revolts that weakened the empire.

**Trap:** Option D (1, 2 and 3) is the trap for students who know that Shah Jahan was architecturally glorious and assume the "most popular emperor" claim must therefore also be true. Option B flips the correctness and includes the false statement 3.

**See also:** [[../../history/14 - Mughal Empire.md#Content|Mughal Empire — source chapter]]
</details>

@qhis.mughal_empire.q12 difficulty=tricky subject=history chapter=14
Who composed the "Akbarnama" and "Ain-i-Akbari"?

- A) Abul Fazl
- B) Faizi
- C) Birbal
- D) Tansen
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

Abul Fazl (1551-1602 CE), the court historian and one of Akbar's "Nine Jewels" (Navaratnas), was the author of both the Akbarnama (a three-volume chronicle of Akbar's reign) and the Ain-i-Akbari (the third volume of the Akbarnama, which serves as a detailed administrative and cultural manual of Akbar's empire). Faizi (option B) was Abul Fazl's elder brother, also a Navaratna, who translated Sanskrit works into Persian and was a poet, but he did not compose these specific texts. Birbal (option C) was another Navaratna, famous as a witty courtier and military commander, but not a historian. Tansen (option D) was the legendary court musician, also a Navaratna, but composed dhrupads and ragas, not historical chronicles.

**Trap:** Options B, C and D are all "Nine Jewels" of Akbar's court, so all four answer choices are real Mughal-era figures associated with Akbar; the question requires students to identify the specific scholar-historian among the Navaratnas. Students who know the phrase "Nine Jewels" but not each member's work will be misled.
