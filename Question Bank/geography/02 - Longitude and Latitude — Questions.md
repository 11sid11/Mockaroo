@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q1 difficulty=recall subject=geography chapter=2
Which of the following latitudes is the largest (longest) parallel of latitude on Earth?

- A) Tropic of Cancer
- B) Equator
- C) Arctic Circle
- D) Antarctic Circle
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The Equator (0°) is the longest parallel of latitude and is the only latitude that is also a great circle, measuring about 40,075 km. All other parallels shrink in length as they move toward the poles.

**Trap:** Students often think the Tropic of Cancer is the largest because it is 'important'; in fact, latitude length is maximum at the Equator and decreases with latitude.

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#Latitude]]
</details>

@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q2 difficulty=recall subject=geography chapter=2
Through which country does the Prime Meridian (0° longitude) officially pass?

- A) Paris, France
- B) Greenwich, United Kingdom
- C) Washington, D.C., USA
- D) Geneva, Switzerland
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The Prime Meridian passes through Greenwich, a district in London, UK. It was adopted as the international standard at the International Meridian Conference in 1884.

**Trap:** Students confuse the Prime Meridian (a longitude of 0°) with the Equator (a latitude of 0°); the Prime Meridian is named after Greenwich, UK.

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#Longitude]]
</details>

@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q3 difficulty=recall subject=geography chapter=2
On approximately which date is the Earth at perihelion (closest to the Sun)?

- A) 21 June
- B) 4 July
- C) 3 January
- D) 22 December
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Earth reaches perihelion around 3 January, when it is about 147 million km from the Sun. Aphelion (farthest, about 152 million km) occurs around 4 July.

**Trap:** Many students assume Earth is closest to the Sun in summer (June) because it is hot; in fact, the Northern Hemisphere is tilted away at perihelion.

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#Distance of Earth from Sun and Moon]]
</details>

@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q4 difficulty=recall subject=geography chapter=2
How many degrees of longitude correspond to a one-hour difference in local time?

- A) 1°
- B) 10°
- C) 15°
- D) 30°
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Earth rotates 360° in 24 hours, so 360° / 24 = 15° per hour. Therefore, 1° of longitude equals 4 minutes of time difference.

**Trap:** Option A (1°) is the time for 4 minutes, not 1 hour — students often confuse degree-to-hour conversion with degree-to-minute conversion.

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#Time]]
</details>

@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q5 difficulty=apply subject=geography chapter=2
A ship sails across the International Date Line from west to east (e.g., from Tokyo toward San Francisco). What happens to the calendar date?

- A) The date advances by 1 day (skip a day)
- B) The date goes back by 1 day (repeat a day)
- C) The date remains unchanged
- D) The date advances by 12 hours
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Crossing the International Date Line from west to east subtracts one day (the traveller 'loses' a day and repeats the calendar date). Crossing from east to west adds one day.

**Trap:** Many students get the direction reversed; the rule is: west to east loses a day, east to west gains a day.

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#International Date Line]]
</details>

@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q6 difficulty=apply subject=geography chapter=2
If it is 12:00 noon at Greenwich (0°), what will be the local time at a place located at 75°E longitude?

- A) 15:00 (3 PM)
- B) 17:00 (5 PM)
- C) 07:00 (7 AM)
- D) 10:00 (10 AM)
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

75°E is 75° east of Greenwich. Time east of Greenwich is ahead. 75° / 15° per hour = 5 hours. So 12:00 + 5 hours = 17:00 (5 PM).

**Trap:** Students forget the direction (east is ahead) and sometimes subtract instead of add, getting 7 AM, which is wrong.

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#Time]]
</details>

@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q7 difficulty=apply subject=geography chapter=2
A traveller stands at the North Pole (90°N). Which of the following statements about longitude is correct for his location?

- A) All longitudes meet at the pole, so any choice of longitude is valid
- B) He is exactly at 0° longitude
- C) He is exactly at 180° longitude
- D) Longitude is undefined at the pole because meridians converge
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

All lines of longitude (meridians) converge at the poles. At the North Pole, every meridian (0°, 90°E, 180°, etc.) meets, so any longitude reference is valid — there is no single fixed longitude at the pole.

**Trap:** Option D sounds logical but is wrong: longitude is not 'undefined' but is effectively all values at once; the pole has no single fixed longitude.

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#Longitude]]
</details>

@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q8 difficulty=apply subject=geography chapter=2
Which latitude divides the Earth into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres, and which meridian divides it into Eastern and Western Hemispheres?

- A) 23½°N; 0° meridian
- B) 0° (Equator); 180° meridian
- C) 0° (Equator); 0° meridian
- D) 0° (Equator); 90°E/90°W pair
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

The Equator (0° latitude) divides Earth into Northern and Southern Hemispheres. The Prime Meridian (0° longitude) and 180° meridian together divide Earth into Eastern and Western Hemispheres — the formal divider is the 0° meridian, with the 180° as its counterpart.

**Trap:** Option B says 180° meridian — but 180° is the antimeridian of 0°, and the conventional divider is the 0° meridian (with 180° as its pair).

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#Hemisphere]]
</details>

@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q9 difficulty=apply subject=geography chapter=2
What is the approximate distance represented by 1° of latitude along any meridian?

- A) 69 km
- B) 111 km
- C) 151 km
- D) 211 km
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

1° of latitude is approximately 111 km (more precisely 110.6 km at the equator, increasing slightly toward the poles due to Earth's flattening). 1° of longitude, by contrast, varies with latitude — maximum at the equator and 0 at the poles.

**Trap:** Option A (69 km) is the distance for 1° of longitude only at the equator; 1° latitude is always near 111 km and is the standard value.

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#Latitude]]
</details>

@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q10 difficulty=tricky subject=geography chapter=2
A pilot flies due east along the 60°N parallel from London (0°) to a point 90° east. Compared to the same easting along the equator, the actual ground distance covered is:

- A) The same as on the equator
- B) Half that on the equator
- C) Twice that on the equator
- D) One-fourth of that on the equator
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The circumference of a parallel at 60°N is half that of the equator (cos 60° = 0.5). So 90° of longitude at 60°N covers exactly half the ground distance of 90° at the equator.

**Trap:** Students often assume all 90° arcs of longitude are equal; in reality, longitude distance shrinks as cos(latitude) of the parallel.

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#Longitude]]
</details>

@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q11 difficulty=tricky subject=geography chapter=2
On 21 June, the sun's vertical rays fall on the Tropic of Cancer. Which of the following correctly describes the day at the North Pole on this date?

- A) Six months of continuous darkness
- B) Six months of continuous daylight begins
- C) Normal 12-hour day like the equator
- D) Sun rises for the first time in the year
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

21 June (Summer Solstice) marks the start of six months of continuous daylight at the North Pole because the North Pole is tilted maximally toward the Sun. Conversely, 22 December marks the start of six months of continuous daylight at the South Pole.

**Trap:** Option D is tempting because the North Pole is associated with the 'first sunrise' but that occurs at the Arctic Circle, not the pole itself.

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#Solstice]]
</details>

@qgeo.longitude_latitude.q12 difficulty=tricky subject=geography chapter=2
Which of the following statements correctly distinguishes a Solar Eclipse from a Lunar Eclipse?

- A) Solar Eclipse occurs on a full moon; Lunar Eclipse on a new moon
- B) Solar Eclipse occurs on a new moon; Lunar Eclipse on a full moon
- C) Both occur only on full moons
- D) Both occur only on new moons
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

A Solar Eclipse occurs when the Moon comes between the Earth and the Sun, casting a shadow on Earth — this happens at New Moon (Amavasya). A Lunar Eclipse occurs when Earth is between the Sun and Moon — at Full Moon (Purnima).

**Trap:** Students often swap these — the key is: Solar (Sun blocked) is New Moon (Moon between Earth and Sun); Lunar (Moon blocked) is Full Moon (Earth between Sun and Moon).

**See also:** [[../../geography/02 - Longitude and Latitude#Solar Eclipse]]
</details>
