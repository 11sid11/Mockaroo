@qchem.matter.q1 difficulty=recall subject=chemistry chapter=1
A research lab cools a dilute gas of rubidium atoms almost to **0 K**. According to the chapter, the exotic state of matter that forms at this temperature is called:

- A) Plasma
- B) Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC)
- C) Supercritical fluid
- D) Fermionic condensate
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

A **Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC)** is formed when a gas of bosons is cooled to temperatures very close to absolute zero (0 K, −273.15 °C). The term "boson" is named after Dr. Satyendra Nath Bose, whose 1924 paper was extended by Albert Einstein in 1925; the first experimental BEC was produced in 1995.

**Trap:** A (plasma) is the *fourth* state, formed by heating a gas to extremely high temperatures, not by cooling. C (supercritical fluid) is a high-temperature/high-pressure state. D (fermionic condensate) is a different phenomenon outside the chapter's listing.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC)]]
</details>

@qchem.matter.q2 difficulty=recall subject=chemistry chapter=1
Who is credited with the **first experimentally produced** Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC), and in which year was it produced?

- A) Satyendra Nath Bose, 1924
- B) Albert Einstein, 1925
- C) Eric Cornell and Carl Wieman, 1995
- D) Wolfgang Ketterle, 2001
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

The **first** BEC was produced experimentally in **1995** by **Eric Cornell and Carl Wieman** using a rarefied gas of rubidium atoms at extremely low temperatures. Wolfgang Ketterle also produced a BEC in 1995 (using sodium atoms), and all three shared the **Nobel Prize in Physics in 2001**.

**Trap:** A and B predicted BEC theoretically (Bose in 1924, Einstein in 1925) — they did not produce it. D is the prize year, not the BEC year, and Ketterle's BEC was with sodium, not rubidium.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#First Production of Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC)]]
</details>

@qchem.matter.q3 difficulty=apply subject=chemistry chapter=1
A sealed syringe holds 20 mL of air at 1 atm pressure. The plunger is pushed in so that the gas is compressed to 10 mL while the temperature stays the same. According to the chapter, the new pressure reading should be closest to:

- A) 0.5 atm
- B) 1 atm
- C) 2 atm
- D) 4 atm
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Boyle's Law states that pressure and volume are **inversely proportional** at constant temperature (P ∝ 1/V; P₁V₁ = P₂V₂). Halving the volume (20 mL → 10 mL) must double the pressure (1 atm → 2 atm).

**Trap:** A is the half that would come from pressure being directly proportional to volume. B ignores the change. D uses a 4× change instead of 2×.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#Boyle's Law (1662)]]
</details>

@qchem.matter.q4 difficulty=recall subject=chemistry chapter=1
Which of the following is an example of **sublimation** (a solid changing directly into a gas)?

- A) Melting of ice into water
- B) Evaporation of nail polish remover
- C) Naphthalene balls disappearing in open air
- D) Formation of dew on a cold surface
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Sublimation is the process of changing a solid **directly into a gas** without passing through a liquid state. The chapter lists **naphthalene** and **dry ice** as classic examples. A is melting (solid → liquid), B is evaporation (liquid → vapour), and D is condensation (vapour → liquid).

**Trap:** D is **deposition** (gas → solid), the opposite of sublimation, and is explicitly listed as an example of deposition in the source.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#Change in State of Matter]]
</details>

@qchem.matter.q5 difficulty=apply subject=chemistry chapter=1
A soda bottle is left open on a hot, windy day. The rate of evaporation of the liquid is **highest** under which set of conditions?

- A) Low temperature, low surface area, low wind speed, high humidity
- B) High temperature, high surface area, high wind speed, low humidity
- C) High temperature, small surface area, no wind, high humidity
- D) High temperature, small surface area, high wind speed, high humidity
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

Per the chapter, the rate of evaporation **increases** when **temperature increases**, **surface area increases**, and **wind speed increases**; it **decreases** when **humidity increases**. So the maximum rate requires high temperature, high surface area, high wind speed, and low humidity → B.

**Trap:** D gets temperature and wind right but picks high humidity, which slows evaporation. C reverses surface area and humidity.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#Favourable Conditions for Evaporation]]
</details>

@qchem.matter.q6 difficulty=apply subject=chemistry chapter=1
The "Tyndall effect" is the most reliable way to distinguish between which two of the following?

- A) Element and compound
- B) Solution and colloid
- C) Homogeneous and heterogeneous mixtures
- D) Solid and liquid
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The chapter states that a solution **does not scatter light** and its particles are less than 1 nm, while a **colloid does scatter light** (Tyndall effect). This is the standard textbook test to tell a colloid from a true solution.

**Trap:** C is tempting because colloids look "mixed," but a homogeneous mixture can still be a **solution** (no Tyndall effect) and a colloid can be homogeneous in appearance too — the scattering of light is what distinguishes them.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#Properties of Solution]]
</details>

@qchem.matter.q7 difficulty=apply subject=chemistry chapter=1
Robert Brown observed a "random zig-zag movement of particles suspended in a medium" in 1827. This motion is known as:

- A) Brownian movement
- B) Tyndall effect
- C) Effervescence
- D) Sedimentation
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

Brownian movement is the **random zig-zag movement of particles suspended in a medium**, caused by collisions with other fast-moving particles in the fluid. It is named after Robert Brown, who discovered it in **1827**.

**Trap:** B (Tyndall effect) is scattering of light by colloidal particles, not the motion of particles. C and D are unrelated phenomena.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#Brownian Movement]]
</details>

@qchem.matter.q8 difficulty=apply subject=chemistry chapter=1
A student dissolves salt in water to recover the salt later. Which separation technique should she use, and which physical property does it exploit?

- A) Filtration — difference in particle size
- B) Evaporation — difference in volatility (salt is non-volatile, water is volatile)
- C) Sublimation — solid turning directly into vapour
- D) Separating funnel — immiscibility of the two liquids
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

**Evaporation** is described as "a process that is used to separate a mixture of a non-volatile and a volatile substance." Salt is non-volatile, water is volatile, so evaporating the water leaves the salt behind. The chapter explicitly lists "separating salt from water" as an application.

**Trap:** A would not work because salt fully dissolves (no solid residue to filter). C requires the solid to itself sublime (like naphthalene). D requires two immiscible liquids.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#Other Methods of Separation of components of Mixture]]
</details>

@qchem.matter.q9 difficulty=apply subject=chemistry chapter=1
A burning candle is described in the chapter as an example of **both** a physical and a chemical change. Which of the following correctly identifies each part?

- A) Thread — physical change; Wax — chemical change
- B) Thread — chemical change; Wax — physical change
- C) Both thread and wax — chemical change only
- D) Both thread and wax — physical change only
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The chapter explicitly states: "Thread: The thread undergoes **chemical change**, as it cannot be obtained again once burnt. Wax: The wax undergoes **physical change**, as the wax can be used to make a new candle again." So the same flame produces two different categories of change at once.

**Trap:** A merely swaps the two. C and D deny the dual nature of the wax or the thread respectively.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#Physical Change vs Chemical Change]]
</details>

@qchem.matter.q10 difficulty=tricky subject=chemistry chapter=1
A student is asked: "Plasma is the 4th state of matter." Which of the following statements about plasma is **incorrect**?

- A) Plasma is an ionised gas
- B) Plasma has roughly equal numbers of positively and negatively charged particles
- C) Plasma can be produced in the laboratory by heating a gas to extremely high temperatures
- D) Plasma is the most common state of matter in the universe because stars are made of it
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: D**

The chapter states that plasma is the 4th state of matter, is an ionised gas with roughly equal positive and negative charges, can be produced by heating a gas to extremely high temperature, and that stars are made of it. While D's first claim ("stars are made of it") is true, the framing "**most common state of matter in the universe**" is **not** something the chapter claims — the chapter only states stars are made of plasma, not that plasma is the most abundant state. A, B, and C are all directly supported.

**Trap:** D reads as a plausible "fun fact" extension, but the source is silent on "most common in the universe." Stick to what the chapter actually says.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#Plasma]]
</details>

@qchem.matter.q11 difficulty=tricky subject=chemistry chapter=1
Consider the following statements about the first experimental Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC):

1. The first BEC was produced in 1995 by Eric Cornell and Carl Wieman using a gas of rubidium atoms.
2. Wolfgang Ketterle also produced a BEC in 1995, but used a gas of sodium atoms.
3. The trio of Cornell, Wieman, and Ketterle shared the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work.

Which of the following is correct?

- A) Only statements 1 and 2 are true
- B) Only statements 2 and 3 are true
- C) Statements 1, 2, and 3 are all true
- D) Only statement 1 is true
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

All three statements are supported by the chapter. The first BEC was produced by **Eric Cornell and Carl Wieman** in **1995** using **rubidium**. **Wolfgang Ketterle** also produced a BEC in 1995, but his used **sodium**. All three shared the **2001 Nobel Prize in Physics**.

**Trap:** A common trap is to deny statement 3 (the prize year is often confused with the BEC year). Another trap is to swap the two elements — but the chapter explicitly attributes rubidium to Cornell–Wieman and sodium to Ketterle.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#First Production of Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC)]]
</details>

@qchem.matter.q12 difficulty=tricky subject=chemistry chapter=1
Consider the following statements about mixtures and solutions:
1. Air is a homogeneous mixture of gases containing about 21% oxygen and 78% nitrogen.
2. Milk of magnesia is classified as a "sol" (dispersed phase solid, dispersion medium liquid).
3. Fog is an example of an aerosol (liquid dispersed in gas).

Which of the following is correct?

- A) Only statement 1 is true
- B) Statements 1 and 2 are true; statement 3 is false
- C) Statements 1, 2, and 3 are all true
- D) Statement 1 is false; statements 2 and 3 are true
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

All three statements are supported by the chapter:
- Air is a homogeneous gas-in-gas mixture, with O₂ (~21%) and N₂ (~78%) as the two main constituents.
- Milk of magnesia is listed as a **sol** (Solid dispersed phase, Liquid dispersion medium).
- Fog is listed as an **aerosol** (Liquid dispersed phase, Gas dispersion medium).

**Trap:** A single statement being "false" is the lure — students sometimes misremember milk of magnesia as an emulsion or fog as a foam, but the chapter's colloid table is explicit.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#Properties of Solution]] · [[../../chemistry/01 - Matter#Colloids]]
</details>
