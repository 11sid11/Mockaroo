@qchem.atom.q1 difficulty=recall subject=chemistry chapter=2
The Law of Conservation of Mass was given by:

- A) Joseph Proust
- B) Antoine Lavoisier
- C) John Dalton
- D) J. J. Thomson
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The chapter states that the Law of Conservation of Mass — "in a closed system, the total mass of a system remains constant over time, meaning mass is neither created nor destroyed, but can only change form" — was given by **Antoine Lavoisier**.

**Trap:** A (Proust) gave the Law of Constant Proportion, not Conservation of Mass. C (Dalton) proposed the atomic theory. D (Thomson) discovered the electron.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/02 - Atom and Its Structure#Law of Conservation of Mass]]
</details>

The Law of Constant Proportion — that a given chemical compound always contains its component elements in a fixed ratio by mass — was proposed by:

- A) Antoine Lavoisier
- B) Joseph Proust

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

The Law of Constant Proportion was given by **Joseph Proust**. The chapter explicitly distinguishes it from Lavoisier's Law of Conservation of Mass.

@qchem.atom.q3 difficulty=recall subject=chemistry chapter=2
Who discovered the electron, and in which year?

- A) Ernest Rutherford, 1911
- B) J. J. Thomson, 1897
- C) Niels Bohr, 1913
- D) James Chadwick, 1932
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

**J. J. Thomson** discovered the electron in **1897** by performing the cathode-ray experiment, observing streams of particles emitted by the cathode of a vacuum tube.

**Trap:** A is the Gold Foil / nuclear-atom model (1911). C is Bohr's quantised-shell model (1913). D is the neutron (1932).

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/02 - Atom and Its Structure#J. J. Thomson and Discovery of Electron (1897)]]
</details>

The neutron was discovered by:

- A) Goldstein
- B) J. J. Thomson
- C) Rutherford
- D) James Chadwick
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: D**

The chapter's table of subatomic particle discoveries lists the **neutron** as discovered by **Chadwick in 1932**. Goldstein and Rutherford are credited with the proton (separately in the text).

**Trap:** A (Goldstein) is associated with the proton — and the chapter offers Rutherford as a co-credit for the proton. C (Rutherford) proposed the nuclear model and named the proton in 1920, but the neutron is Chadwick's.

</details>

@qchem.atom.q5 difficulty=apply subject=chemistry chapter=2
The maximum number of electrons that can be accommodated in the **M-shell** (n = 3) of an atom is:

- A) 6
- B) 8
- C) 18
- D) 32
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

The chapter gives the formula **2n²** for the maximum number of electrons in the nth shell. For the M-shell (n = 3): 2 × 3² = 2 × 9 = **18**.

**Trap:** B (8) is for the L-shell (n = 2). D (32) is for the N-shell (n = 4). A (6) is not a standard 2n² value.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/02 - Atom and Its Structure#Distribution of Electrons (e⁻)]]
</details>

@qchem.atom.q6 difficulty=apply subject=chemistry chapter=2
An atom of sodium (Na) has 11 electrons. How many **protons** and **neutrons** does it have, given its mass number is 23?

- A) 11 protons, 12 neutrons
- B) 12 protons, 11 neutrons
- C) 11 protons, 11 neutrons
- D) 23 protons, 11 neutrons
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: A**

For a neutral atom, the number of protons equals the number of electrons, so Na has **11 protons**. The mass number A = p + n, so neutrons = A − p = 23 − 11 = **12 neutrons**.

**Trap:** B swaps p and n. C uses A = electrons (gives wrong n). D treats mass number as the proton count.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/02 - Atom and Its Structure#Atomic Number (Z)]] · [[../../chemistry/02 - Atom and Its Structure#Mass Number (A)]]
</details>

@qchem.atom.q7 difficulty=apply subject=chemistry chapter=2
Isotopes of the same element have **identical** chemical properties because:

- A) They have the same number of neutrons
- B) They have the same mass number
- C) They have the same electronic configuration and the same number of valence electrons
- D) They have the same number of neutrons and the same mass number
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

The chapter states: "Isotopes have same electronic configuration, hence same number of valance e⁻. Since chemical property depends upon the number of valance e⁻. Therefore, they have same chemical properties." Isotopes differ only in the number of neutrons, so A is wrong; their mass numbers differ, so B is wrong; D is factually wrong on the second clause.

**Trap:** A is true about the *difference* between isotopes (they differ only in n), but it is the *same valence e⁻* that explains identical chemical behaviour.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/02 - Atom and Its Structure#ISOTOPES]]
</details>

@qchem.atom.q8 difficulty=apply subject=chemistry chapter=2
Isotopes of an element have **different physical properties** because:

- A) They have different atomic numbers
- B) They have different electron counts
- C) They have different masses, leading to different melting points, boiling points, and densities
- D) They have different chemical properties
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

The chapter states: "Since, the isotopes of an element have different masses, hence different physical properties, like melting point, boiling point, density, etc. exist." Isotopes have the **same** atomic number (A) and **same** electron count (B), and have **same** chemical properties (D).

**Trap:** A and B are wrong because isotopes have the same Z and the same electron count. D is the opposite of the truth — isotopes share their chemical properties.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/02 - Atom and Its Structure#ISOTOPES]]
</details>

@qchem.atom.q9 difficulty=apply subject=chemistry chapter=2
Which of the following sets are **isobars** — atoms of different elements with the same mass number but different atomic numbers?

- A) ¹H, ²H, ³H
- B) ¹²C, ¹³C, ¹⁴C
- C) ⁴⁰Ar, ⁴⁰K, ⁴⁰Ca
- D) ¹⁶O, ¹⁵N
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

Per the chapter, isobars "are atoms of different elements that have same mass number, but different atomic numbers." ⁴⁰₁₈Ar, ⁴⁰₁₉K, and ⁴⁰₂₀Ca are explicitly listed as the example, with each having 40 as the mass number but different atomic numbers (18, 19, 20).

**Trap:** A are isotopes of hydrogen (same Z = 1, different A). B are isotopes of carbon. D are **isotones** (same neutron count = 8, different Z = 8 and 7).

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/02 - Atom and Its Structure#ISOBARS]]
</details>

@qchem.atom.q10 difficulty=tricky subject=chemistry chapter=2
The atomic mass of chlorine is reported as 35.5. Based on the chapter's worked example, this is because chlorine consists of two isotopes, Cl-35 and Cl-37, present in the ratio:

- A) 1 : 1
- B) 1 : 3
- C) 3 : 1
- D) 2 : 1
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

The chapter gives the worked example: "³⁵₁₇Cl : ³⁷₁₇Cl in ratio 3:1, Atomic Mass = (35 × 3 + 37 × 1) / (3 + 1) = 35.5". So chlorine exists as Cl-35 and Cl-37 in the ratio **3 : 1**.

**Trap:** A (1:1) would give an average of 36, not 35.5. B (1:3) would give (35 + 3 × 37)/4 = 36.5. D (2:1) gives (70 + 37)/3 = 35.67.

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/02 - Atom and Its Structure#ISOTOPES]]
</details>

@qchem.atom.q11 difficulty=tricky subject=chemistry chapter=2
In Bohr's model of the atom (1913), which of the following statements is **correct**?

- A) Electrons continuously lose energy as they revolve around the nucleus
- B) Energy levels are labeled K, L, M, N, with K being the outermost shell
- C) The ground state refers to the electron being in the K-shell (the innermost shell)
- D) When an electron falls from a lower to a higher energy level, it emits energy
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: C**

The chapter lists three attributes of Bohr's model: (1) electrons revolve in stable orbits **without losing energy**, (2) the shells are labeled K, L, M, N with **K being the closest to the nucleus**, and (3) the **ground state is the lowest energy level, typically in the K-shell**. A is the opposite of (1) — that's why Bohr replaced Rutherford's model. B reverses K's position. D reverses the emission rule (falling from higher → lower emits energy; lower → higher absorbs energy).

**Trap:** A is the classic Rutherford-model flaw that Bohr fixed. D is the most subtle trap — it reverses "absorbs" and "emits."

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/02 - Atom and Its Structure#Bohr's Model of Atom (1913)]]
</details>

@qchem.atom.q12 difficulty=tricky subject=chemistry chapter=2
J. J. Thomson proposed the first atomic model in 1904 — the "Plum Pudding Model." Which of the following correctly describes it?

- A) A dense, positively charged nucleus with electrons revolving around it in circular orbits
- B) A positively charged sphere in which the electrons are embedded, with equal positive and negative charges making the atom electrically neutral
- C) A nucleus containing protons and neutrons, with electrons arranged in fixed energy shells K, L, M, N
- D) A central nucleus with discrete energy levels where electrons neither absorb nor emit energy while orbiting
@@

<details><summary>Answer & explanation</summary>

**Answer: B**

The chapter states: Thomson proposed that (i) "an atom consists of a positively charged sphere and the electrons are embedded in it" and (ii) "the negative and positive charges are equal in magnitude. So, the atom as a whole is electrically neutral." He called this the Plum Pudding / Raisin Pudding / Watermelon model.

**Trap:** A is **Rutherford's** model (dense nucleus + orbiting electrons). C mixes Rutherford's nucleus with **Bohr's** shells. D is a corrupted Bohr statement (Bohr's electrons *don't lose energy* but they *can* absorb/emit when changing levels).

**See also:** [[../../chemistry/02 - Atom and Its Structure#J. J. Thomson and Discovery of Electron (1897)]]
</details>
